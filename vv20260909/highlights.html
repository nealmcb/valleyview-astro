Why these two nights are special

No Moon. New Moon is Sept 11, so both nights are essentially moonless — under Valley View’s Bortle 2 skies that’s about as dark as the sky ever gets.

The sky keeps getting better through the evening as the summer Milky Way swings higher.

Naked eye — start here

  • The Milky Way, horizon to horizon. Once it’s dark it arches across the whole sky: the bright star clouds of Sagittarius low in the southwest (you’re looking toward the center of our galaxy), straight up through Cygnus overhead, and down into Cassiopeia in the northeast. Look for the Great Rift — the dark lane of dust splitting the glow in two. On a night this dark it can be bright enough to cast a faint shadow.

  • The Summer Triangle, nearly overhead: Vega, Deneb, Altair. Same little triangle, wildly different distances — Altair 17 light-years, Vega 25, Deneb over 1,500.

  • Andromeda Galaxy (M31) clears the eastern ridge by mid-evening — the farthest thing visible to the unaided eye, 2.5 million light-years away, and several times wider than the full Moon.

  • Venus, brilliant but very low in the west — it sets less than 1½ hours after the Sun. Catch it in binoculars right at dusk or not at all.

In the telescope

  • Ring Nebula (M57) — almost straight up all evening. A blue-green smoke ring: the puffed-off outer layers of a dying star.

  • Albireo — the classic double star in Cygnus, a gold star and a blue one side by side.

  • M13 — the great globular cluster in Hercules, high in the west; a few hundred thousand stars in one ball.

  • Saturn rises in the southeast around 8:30 p.m. and is well up by 10–10:30. Rings (tilted only a few degrees now) plus its big moon Titan. Faint blue Neptune is in the same part of Pisces — a tiny dot in the eyepiece.
