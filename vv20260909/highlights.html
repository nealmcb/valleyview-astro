Why these two nights are special

No Moon. New Moon falls on Sept 10, so on both nights the Moon is gone before the sky is fully dark — under Valley View’s Bortle 2 skies that’s about as dark as it ever gets.

The sky keeps getting better through the evening as the summer Milky Way swings higher.

Naked eye — start here

  • The Milky Way, horizon to horizon. Once it’s dark it arches across the whole sky: the bright star clouds of Sagittarius low in the southwest (you’re looking toward the center of our galaxy), straight up through Cygnus overhead, and down into Cassiopeia in the northeast. Look for the Great Rift — the dark lane of dust splitting the glow in two. On a night this dark it can be bright enough to cast a faint shadow.

  • The Summer Triangle, nearly overhead: Vega, Deneb, and Altair. Same little triangle, wildly different distances — Altair 17 light-years, Vega 25, Deneb over 1,500.

  • Andromeda Galaxy (M31) clears the mountains to the east-northeast about 7:50 p.m. and is well up by the time the sky is fully dark. It’s the farthest thing visible to the unaided eye — 2.5 million light-years — and several times wider than the full Moon.

  • Venus, brilliant but very low in the west — it sets less than 1½ hours after the Sun. Catch it in binoculars right at dusk or not at all.

Satellites passing over

Something bright drifts through most clear nights here. The pick of them on Tue Sept 9 (times MDT):

  • The Space Station (ISS) — a brilliant pass at 8:03 p.m., magnitude −3.8 (brighter than anything else in the sky): up from the southwest, 66° high in the southeast, fading out in the northeast. It swings back lower and dimmer at 9:41.
  • Tiangong, China’s space station — 8:54 p.m., magnitude −2.2, from the west-northwest to 74° up in the south.
  • In between, a steady trickle of spent rocket bodies (the tumbling SL‑16 Zenit upper stages sometimes flare) and reconnaissance satellites at 2nd–3rd magnitude — see the table below.

In the telescope

  • Ring Nebula (M57) — almost straight up all evening. A blue-green smoke ring: the puffed-off outer layers of a dying star.

  • Albireo — the classic double star in Cygnus, a gold star and a blue one side by side.

  • M13 — the great globular cluster in Hercules, high in the west; a few hundred thousand stars in one ball.

  • Saturn clears the ridge around 9:45 p.m. and is well up in the southeast by 11. The rings are nearly edge-on right now — a thin bright line — plus its big moon Titan. Faint blue Neptune sits about a fist-width away in Pisces for anyone who wants one more planet in the eyepiece.
